# Micro-SaaS Scout — Strategy

## Strategic shift

The previous project `nishe_scout` focused heavily on marketplace discovery and repeated competitor/keyword research.

This project changes the order of operations.

### New model

`Pain → Existing spending → Existing solution → Dissatisfaction → Wedge → Distribution → Validation → Build`

The central constraint is no cold outreach, not no marketing.

## Acquisition principle

The product should have a plausible path to inbound discovery.

Allowed channels include:

- marketplace search;
- web search / SEO;
- niche communities;
- directories;
- comparison pages;
- educational content;
- public tools/calculators/templates;
- Product Hunt-style launches;
- social content that attracts problem-aware users;
- integrations/ecosystems with built-in discovery;
- partner/referral mechanisms that do not depend on mass cold outreach.

The channel must fit the problem. We do not choose a channel merely because it is available.

## Opportunity criteria

A candidate becomes interesting when most of the following are true:

### Problem
The problem is concrete, repeated, and costly enough to matter.

### Buyer
There is a clearly identifiable person or role who owns the problem.

### Existing spending
The buyer already spends money, labor, time, or workaround effort to handle it.

### Dissatisfaction
There is observable dissatisfaction with the current approach.

### Wedge
A small product can solve one important part of the problem substantially better.

### Distribution
A solo founder can plausibly put the product in front of problem-aware users through an inbound or ecosystem channel.

### Payment
A cheap experiment can test whether the buyer will actually pay.

### Feasibility
The first version can be built and operated by one developer without disproportionate support burden.

## Validation hierarchy

Prefer evidence in roughly this order:

1. Actual payment.
2. Pre-order / paid pilot / deposit.
3. Strong request from an identifiable buyer plus clear existing spend.
4. Repeated direct evidence of comparison or switching.
5. Repeated workaround behavior.
6. Repeated problem reports with buying context.
7. Search/discovery interest.
8. General opinions or theoretical market size.

A weaker signal cannot compensate for failure at a higher-order gate.

## Business-model boundaries

Avoid, unless evidence is exceptional:

- products requiring large outbound sales teams;
- enterprise products with long procurement cycles;
- markets where trust/security certification dominates initial sales;
- generic AI wrappers without a differentiated workflow;
- products whose economics depend on large paid-ad budgets;
- ideas where a major platform can easily ship the whole wedge natively.

## Validation rule

The first experiment should answer a commercial question, not a technical question.

Examples:

- Will someone pay for this?
- Will someone leave a deposit?
- Will problem-aware users request access?
- Will people sign up after seeing the specific solution?
- Will a marketplace or search listing generate qualified discovery?

Do not start by asking:

> "Can we build it?"

First ask:

> "Can we get evidence that somebody wants to buy it?"
